## Introduction

Paperguide is an all-in-one AI research platform built for researchers, academics, and students. It helps you move from discovery to analysis to writing without splitting your work across disconnected tools.

You can use Paperguide to:

- Search across a large academic corpus for relevant literature
- Upload and analyze papers in a document-grounded workspace
- Generate literature reviews with a structured workflow
- Screen, extract, and synthesize evidence across studies
- Chat with PDFs and trace answers back to cited passages
- Organize references and reuse papers across workflows
- Draft and refine academic writing with AI support

Paperguide uses a workspace model built around **organizations** and **workspaces**. Organizations group people and shared activity, while workspaces keep a specific project, topic, team, or review effort organized in one place.

That structure matters because Paperguide's AI agents can work with the context already present in your workspace. Papers, notes, and prior work become part of the environment rather than isolated inputs you need to reassemble each time.

<Callout kind="info">
  A practical way to think about Paperguide is as one research environment with multiple specialized assistants rather than a collection of disconnected tools.
</Callout>

## AI Search

AI Search helps you move quickly from a research question to a usable evidence set. It is designed for literature discovery, not general web search.

AI Search returns:

- A synthesized answer to your research question
- The 20 most relevant papers for that question
- A reusable reference list you can carry into later work

Paperguide runs AI Search across more than 200 million peer-reviewed papers. You can also narrow the search scope when you want a more targeted result set, including sources such as **PubMed**, **arXiv**, or your **personal library**.

### When to use AI Search

Start with AI Search when you need to:

- Get oriented on an unfamiliar research topic
- Find a strong starting set of academic papers
- Compare how recent papers frame a question
- Build a reference list before deeper analysis

### When to use a different tool

| You want to | Best fit |
| --- | --- |
| Explore a question through back-and-forth reasoning, analysis, and drafting | Research Agent |
| Produce a structured review workflow with screening and extraction | Literature Review Agent |
| Ask detailed questions about specific documents you already have | Chat with PDF |

### Typical workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open AI Search" icon="search" title-type="p">

    Open the AI Search area from your Paperguide workspace. Start there when your main goal is discovery rather than deep analysis of known documents.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Enter your research question" icon="edit" title-type="p">

    Phrase the question the way you would ask a research assistant. Include the core topic, population, method, timeframe, or comparison if those details matter.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply filters" icon="settings" title-type="p">

    Narrow the scope if you want results from a specific source such as PubMed or arXiv, or if you want the search to focus on your personal library.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Review the answer and evidence set" icon="check-circle" title-type="p">

    Read the synthesized answer first, then inspect the recommended papers behind it. A good result gives you both a usable summary and a paper set worth following.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Open references and follow up" icon="arrow-right" title-type="p">

    Use the reference list to continue into reading, comparison, or drafting. At this stage, many users move into Research Agent, Literature Review Agent, or Chat with PDF depending on the next task.

  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout kind="info">
  AI Search is oriented toward fast, evidence-backed discovery. It does not search general web content such as blogs or non-academic internet sources.
</Callout>

## Research Agent

Research Agent is the most flexible assistant in Paperguide. Use it when your work does not fit neatly into a single narrow workflow and you want help moving between discovery, analysis, synthesis, and writing.

Research Agent can help you:

- Answer research questions
- Find relevant papers
- Compare studies
- Summarize papers
- Identify limitations or research gaps
- Edit and refine academic writing
- Support screening or extraction planning before structured review work

Research Agent works best when your task is exploratory or iterative. If you are thinking through a question, testing angles, comparing evidence, or turning notes into clearer prose, this is usually the best place to begin.

### Typical workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Select Research Agent" icon="book-open" title-type="p">

    Open Research Agent when you want a general-purpose assistant rather than a tool built for one fixed process.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Describe the question and constraints" icon="edit" title-type="p">

    State what you are trying to answer and add constraints such as domain, study type, population, time range, or writing goal.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Add context" icon="plus" title-type="p">

    Bring in supporting materials such as papers, PDFs, notes, or references from your workspace. Better context usually leads to more useful comparisons and summaries.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Iterate between discovery, analysis, and writing" icon="refresh-cw" title-type="p">

    Ask follow-up questions, request comparisons, refine summaries, or reshape output into notes, outlines, or draft language.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Move to specialized tools when needed" icon="arrow-right" title-type="p">

    Shift into Literature Review Agent for structured review production, Chat with PDF for document-grounded reading, or Extract Data for table-based comparison work.

  </Step>
</Steps>

### How it differs from specialized agents

- **Research Agent** is broad and flexible, so it fits mixed workflows and iterative thinking.
- **Literature Review Agent** is structured for review generation with planning, search, screening, extraction, and synthesis stages.
- **Chat with PDF** is optimized for asking questions about specific documents with page-level grounding.
- **Extract Data** is designed for structured comparison and exportable workbooks rather than open-ended conversation.

<Callout kind="info">
  Outputs should always be reviewed directly against cited papers. The agent supports research judgment; it does not replace it.
</Callout>

## Literature Review Agent

Literature Review Agent is designed for review-oriented work where the process matters as much as the final summary. It gives you a structured path from planning through synthesis.

It is designed to help you:

- Plan the scope and structure of a literature review
- Search for and assemble candidate papers
- Screen papers for relevance and inclusion
- Extract findings and synthesize them into a review output

### Inputs you can provide

Literature Review Agent becomes more useful when you start with clear review intent and relevant source material. Common inputs include:

- A research question or review objective
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria
- Topic constraints such as field, timeframe, or study type
- Candidate papers already found through search
- PDFs or references stored in your workspace
- Notes about the outcome or format you want from the review

### Typical workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Plan the review" icon="settings" title-type="p">

    Define the review question, boundaries, and criteria. This stage shapes the quality of every later step.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Search and build candidates" icon="search" title-type="p">

    Gather a candidate set of papers that may belong in the review. The goal here is coverage before final selection.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Screen papers" icon="check-circle" title-type="p">

    Evaluate which papers fit your criteria. Screening outcomes help separate the evidence you will use from the studies you will set aside.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Extract key details" icon="database" title-type="p">

    Pull out the information needed for synthesis, such as methods, outcomes, limitations, or other review-specific details.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Synthesize findings into a review report" icon="file-text" title-type="p">

    Combine the extracted evidence into a structured review output that reflects patterns, differences, and gaps across the literature.

  </Step>
</Steps>

### Outputs

| Output | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Literature review report | A structured summary of findings synthesized across selected papers |
| Candidate and selected papers | The paper set considered during search and screening |
| Screening outcomes | Records of which papers were included or excluded |
| Extracted data | Structured information pulled from selected studies for downstream analysis |

<Callout kind="tip">
  A strong working pattern is to start with Literature Review Agent for speed, then move into workbook-style refinement for deeper manual control.
</Callout>

## Chat with PDF

Chat with PDF helps you ask targeted questions about documents and get answers grounded in the source material. It is especially useful when you already have papers in hand and want to understand them faster without losing traceability.

What makes Chat with PDF useful:

- Answers stay tied to the document rather than drifting into generic summary
- Citations help you verify claims against the original source
- Follow-up questions make iterative reading faster
- It works for both single-document and multi-document analysis

### How to add documents

You can add documents to Chat with PDF in several ways:

- Upload PDF files directly
- Select papers from Reference Manager
- Paste a PDF URL, DOI, or arXiv ID

### Typical workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Add PDFs" icon="upload" title-type="p">

    Bring in one document or a focused set of related papers. Smaller, coherent document sets usually produce clearer answers.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Wait for indexing" icon="check" title-type="p">

    Give Paperguide time to process the documents. Once indexing completes, the PDFs are ready for question answering.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Ask a question" icon="help-circle" title-type="p">

    Ask about methods, findings, limitations, definitions, or differences across papers. Specific questions usually produce stronger responses than broad prompts.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Review inline citations" icon="book-open" title-type="p">

    Check where the answer is grounded in the source. Citations help you confirm that the summary reflects the actual document.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Use follow-up questions" icon="arrow-right" title-type="p">

    Continue the conversation to clarify ambiguous points, compare passages, or narrow the answer to the section you care about most.

  </Step>
</Steps>

### Citation behavior

Citations in Chat with PDF are valuable because they keep the interaction anchored in the source text. In practice, that means you can expect the tool to support:

- Document-grounded answers rather than free-floating summaries
- Inline references to the underlying PDF content
- Traceability back to the source while reviewing claims
- Better verification during close reading and note-taking

Multi-document analysis works well when the documents belong together, such as papers on the same method, outcome, or debate. If the set becomes too broad or mixed, answers can become less focused because the underlying evidence is pulling in different directions.

<Callout kind="info">
  Best results for deep synthesis usually come from focused document sets rather than very large mixed collections.
</Callout>

## Extract Data

Extract Data gives you a structured workspace for comparing papers side by side. It is useful when your work depends on consistency across studies rather than isolated summaries.

### Typical workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open Extract Data" icon="database" title-type="p">

    Start in Extract Data when you want to build a comparison table rather than ask open-ended questions.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Select papers from Reference Manager" icon="book-open" title-type="p">

    Choose the papers you want to compare. Using saved references keeps the workflow connected to the rest of your research workspace.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Create a workbook table" icon="plus" title-type="p">

    Set up a workbook that will hold one row per paper and one column per comparison field.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Add predefined or custom columns" icon="settings" title-type="p">

    Use built-in research fields where they fit, then add custom columns for project-specific criteria or coding schemes.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Review and refine extracted values" icon="edit" title-type="p">

    Inspect the generated values, correct mistakes, and normalize wording so the table stays useful for analysis.

  </Step>
  <Step title="Export the completed workbook" icon="download" title-type="p">

    Export the finished table when you are ready to share it, analyze it elsewhere, or include it in a broader review workflow.

  </Step>
</Steps>

### Column options

Extract Data supports both standard research fields and project-specific columns.

- **Predefined columns** often include key findings, methodology, limitations, conclusions, study outcomes, and demographics.
- **Custom columns** let you capture the criteria that matter for your own review, comparison, or coding framework.

### Why teams use it

Teams often use Extract Data to:

- Standardize how they compare studies
- Reduce copy-paste work across papers
- Keep extraction decisions visible and editable
- Prepare data for export into other analysis workflows

### Export and persistence

Workbooks save automatically as you work, so you do not need to rebuild the table each session. When the workbook is ready, export it to **CSV**, **Excel**, or **BibTeX** depending on how you plan to use the output next.
